# Abstract-Specific Editing Guidelines

**Role in Skill:** This provides EDITING INSTRUCTIONS—how to edit abstracts to meet CNS standards.

**Distinct from:** `abstracts_patterns.md` (PATTERNS extracted from CNS papers as emulation targets)

**When to use:** During abstract editing as step-by-step guidance.

**Target:** 150-250 words for CNS journals (Nature, Cell, Science)

**Purpose:** Provide complete, self-contained summary of the entire study—readable independently from the main text.

---

## Required Structure (8 Essential Parts)

### 1. Opening Context (1-2 sentences)

**Purpose:** Establish broad biological/medical significance

**What to include:**

- Why this biological process/disease/system matters
- General importance to science or health

**Examples from CNS papers:**

- "Aging is associated with stem cell decline..."
- "Iron homeostasis is critical for cellular function..."
- "Transcriptional regulation underlies cell fate decisions..."

---

### 2. Knowledge Gap (1 sentence)

**Purpose:** State what remains unknown or controversial

**Key phrases:**

- "but whether X affects Y remains unclear"
- "However, the mechanism remains poorly understood"
- "yet how X regulates Y is unknown"
- "Nevertheless, whether X is required for Y has not been tested"

**What makes a good gap:**

- ✅ Specific and focused
- ✅ Directly addressed by this study
- ✅ Connects opening context to your work
- ❌ NOT generic ("more research is needed")
- ❌ NOT solved by existing literature

---

### 3. Study Statement (1 sentence)

**Purpose:** Introduce THIS study clearly

**Required phrases:**

- "Here we show..."
- "Here, using [experimental system], we demonstrate..."
- "We now report..."
- "In this study, we reveal..."

**What to include:**

- Experimental system/model
- Main approach or innovation
- Central finding (can be brief, expanded in findings section)

**Example:**

> "Here, using physiologically aged mice, we demonstrate that aging suppresses lung cancer by degrading alveolar type 2 (AT2) cell stemness."

---

### 4. Approach (1 sentence, optional but recommended)

**Purpose:** Briefly describe key methods

**What to include:**

- Major experimental approaches
- Novel techniques or tools
- Model systems

**Keep brief—methods details belong in Methods section**

**Example:**

> "Using single-cell RNA sequencing, genetic lineage tracing, and loss-of-function experiments, we characterized..."

---

### 5. Major Findings (2-3 sentences)

**Purpose:** Present key results with quantitative precision

**CRITICAL requirements:**

- ✅ Specific numbers (fold-changes, percentages, ranges)
- ✅ Statistics (n, P values) for major claims
- ✅ Hierarchical presentation (strongest findings first)
- ✅ Active voice ("we found," "we show")

**Quantification patterns:**

- Fold changes: "increased 2.3-fold," "reduced by 68%"
- Percentages: "47% reduction," "63% ± 23%"
- Comparisons: ">100-fold weaker than..."
- Ranges: "54-73% across all conditions"

**Example:**

> "Aged mice exhibited a 68% reduction in tumor number (n = 8 mice; P = 0.004) and a 40% decrease in tumor volume (P = 0.012) compared with young mice."

**Hierarchy principle:**

- Strongest/most robust findings FIRST
- Partial/weaker findings SECOND
- Use "Although..." or "However..." to introduce partial findings

**Example:**

> "dITP activates KomBC most robustly (Fig. 3A). Although IDP also activated KomBC, it triggered a >100-fold-weaker response compared with dITP, and other nucleotides did not activate KomBC."

---

### 6. Mechanistic Insight (1-2 sentences)

**Purpose:** Explain HOW or WHY the findings occur

**What to include:**

- Molecular pathway or mechanism
- Causal connections between observations
- Biological logic

**Syntactic tools for integration:**

- Participial phrases: "preventing X, leading to Y"
- "by [verb]-ing": "by degrading stemness"
- "through [noun]-mediated": "through ferroptosis-mediated cell death"

**Example:**

> "This phenotype is driven by aging-associated induction of NUPR1 and lipocalin-2, which together cause functional iron insufficiency."

**Bad (mechanism bolted on awkwardly):**

> "Treatment increased expression. The mechanism involves NUPR1. NUPR1 regulates lipocalin-2."

**Good (mechanism integrated):**

> "Treatment increased expression by activating NUPR1, which induces lipocalin-2 and leads to functional iron insufficiency."

---

### 7. Validation (1 sentence, optional)

**Purpose:** Show robustness through multi-method or in vivo confirmation

**When to include:**

- If you validated key finding with second method
- If you confirmed in vivo after in vitro discovery
- If you performed rescue experiments

**Example:**

> "Genetic inactivation of the NUPR1–lipocalin-2 axis rescued stemness by 73% (n = 6 replicates; P = 0.002) and promoted tumorigenesis in aged cells."

---

### 8. Impact Statement (1 sentence)

**Purpose:** State broader implications or applications

**What to include:**

- Field-level impact
- Therapeutic/translational potential
- Conceptual advance

**Appropriate strength:**

- ✅ "These findings have implications for..."
- ✅ "This work reveals a mechanism that may..."
- ✅ "Our results suggest potential strategies for..."
- ❌ NOT "This will revolutionize..." (overclaiming)
- ❌ NOT "More research is needed" (weak ending)

**Example:**

> "These findings reveal that aging-driven iron insufficiency cell-intrinsically suppresses both regenerative capacity and cancer susceptibility, with implications for regenerative medicine and cancer prevention in elderly populations."

---

## Editing Checklist for Abstracts

**Structure:**

- [ ] Opening context (1-2 sentences) present
- [ ] Specific knowledge gap stated (1 sentence)
- [ ] Clear "Here we..." or equivalent study statement (1 sentence)
- [ ] Major findings with quantification (2-3 sentences)
- [ ] Mechanistic insight provided (1-2 sentences)
- [ ] Impact/implications stated (1 sentence)
- [ ] Total: 150-250 words

**Voice and Style:**

- [ ] Active voice predominates ("We found," not "It was found")
- [ ] Declarative statements (confident, not hedging excessively)
- [ ] No citations (abstracts standalone)
- [ ] Technical terms defined or avoided
- [ ] Each sentence advances the narrative (no filler)

**Quantitative Precision:**

- [ ] Specific numbers provided (fold-changes, percentages)
- [ ] Statistics included for major findings (n, P values, test names)
- [ ] NO vague quantifiers ("significantly," "greatly," "many")

**Abbreviations:**

- [ ] Spelled out on first use (then abbreviated)
- [ ] OR avoided if space is tight

**Common Issues to Fix:**

- Passive constructions → Change to active voice
- Vague quantification ("many," "several") → Add specific numbers
- Missing "Here we..." → Add explicit study introduction
- Generic impact → Make specific to field/disease/application
- Too long (>250 words) → Use conciseness techniques (see below)

## Conciseness Techniques for Abstracts

**Challenge:** Fit complete information in 150-250 words

**Solution:** Syntactic sophistication to increase information density

---

### Technique 1: Parallel Structures

**Formula:** `[Method 1] or [method 2] [shared effect] [shared outcome]`

**Example:**

**❌ VERBOSE (44 words):**

> "We genetically inactivated the NUPR1-lipocalin-2 axis in aged alveolar cells, which rescued stemness. We also supplemented aged cells with iron, which promoted tumorigenic potential."

**✅ CONCISE (21 words):**

> "Genetic inactivation of the NUPR1–lipocalin-2 axis or iron supplementation rescued stemness and promoted tumorigenic potential."

**Savings:** 52% reduction, 100% information preserved

**When to use:**

- Multiple interventions with same outcome
- Multiple phenotypes with same cause
- Multiple models showing same result

---

### Technique 2: Participial Phrases

**Formula:** `[Action 1], [verb]-ing [result 1], [verb]-ing [result 2]`

**Example:**

**❌ VERBOSE (19 words):**

> "CDK1 phosphorylates ASB7. This prevents SUV39H1 interaction. This leads to H3K9me3 restoration."

**✅ CONCISE (11 words):**

> "CDK1 phosphorylates ASB7, preventing SUV39H1 interaction, leading to H3K9me3 restoration."

**Savings:** 42% reduction, improved causal flow

**Common participial verbs:**

- **preventing / blocking:** "phosphorylating X, preventing Y binding"
- **leading to / resulting in:** "activating cascade, leading to apoptosis"
- **promoting:** "stabilizing protein, promoting activity"
- **revealing:** "analyzing data, revealing novel pathway"
- **demonstrating:** "performing rescue, demonstrating causality"

---

### Technique 3: Appositive Constructions

**Purpose:** Avoid definition sentences—embed definitions in commas

**Example:**

**❌ VERBOSE (12 words):**

> "TnpB is an RNA-guided DNA endonuclease. TnpB mediates dsDNA cleavage."

**✅ CONCISE (9 words):**

> "TnpB, an RNA-guided DNA endonuclease, mediates dsDNA cleavage."

**Savings:** 25% reduction, smoother flow

**Pattern:**

> [Protein/term], [brief definition], [what it does].

**When to use:**

- First mention of technical terms
- When definition is short (≤5 words)
- When flow is improved by embedding

---

### Technique 4: Compound Subjects/Objects

**Purpose:** Combine related elements to reduce sentence count

**Example:**

**❌ VERBOSE (16 words):**

> "Aging reduces tumor initiation. Aging also reduces tumor progression. Both effects are cell-intrinsic."

**✅ CONCISE (9 words):**

> "Aging cell-intrinsically reduces tumor initiation and progression."

**Savings:** 44% reduction

---

## Word Count Targets (with techniques applied)

| Section             | Target Words | Techniques to Use                      |
| ------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Opening context     | 15-30        | Appositives for term definition        |
| Gap + "Here we..."  | 30-50        | Parallel structures, compound verbs    |
| Major findings      | 40-70        | Participial phrases, compound objects  |
| Mechanism           | 25-40        | Participial phrases, causal connectors |
| Validation + Impact | 30-50        | Parallel structures, appositives       |
| **TOTAL**           | **150-200**  | Combination of all techniques          |

## Abstract Quality Standards (CNS-Level)

**Gate 1: Completeness**

- [ ] Can be understood without reading the paper
- [ ] Tells a complete scientific story
- [ ] All 8 structural elements present

**Gate 2: Precision**

- [ ] Quantification specific (not "significantly increased")
- [ ] Statistics complete where appropriate
- [ ] Claims calibrated to evidence strength

**Gate 3: Clarity**

- [ ] Accessible to scientists outside narrow specialty
- [ ] Technical terms defined or contextualized
- [ ] Logical flow from context → gap → findings → impact

**Gate 4: Voice**

- [ ] Active voice predominant
- [ ] Declarative and confident
- [ ] Concise without being telegraphic

**Gate 5: Impact**

- [ ] Broader implications clear
- [ ] Field-level significance stated
- [ ] Not oversold, not undersold

## Common Abstract Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Vague Opening**

- ❌ "Aging is important."
- ✅ "Aging is associated with stem cell decline, but its effect on tumorigenesis remains unclear."

**Mistake 2: No Clear Gap**

- ❌ "We studied aging and cancer."
- ✅ "However, whether aging suppresses or promotes cancer remains poorly understood."

**Mistake 3: Missing Quantification**

- ❌ "Aged mice had fewer tumors."
- ✅ "Aged mice exhibited a 68% reduction in tumor number (n = 8; P = 0.004)."

**Mistake 4: Weak Conclusion**

- ❌ "This is interesting and more research is needed."
- ✅ "These findings have implications for cancer prevention in elderly populations."

**Mistake 5: Passive Voice**

- ❌ "It was found that protein levels were increased."
- ✅ "We found that protein levels increased by 2.3-fold (P < 0.01)."

## Example Abstract Transformation

### BEFORE (Weak, 51 words)

> "Aging affects stem cells and cancer risk. We looked at lung cells in old and young mice. We found that old mice had fewer tumors. The tumors were smaller. Iron levels might be involved. This could be important for understanding cancer in elderly people."

**Problems:**

- Vague opening
- No specific gap
- No "Here we..." statement
- Missing quantification
- No statistics
- Weak mechanism
- Generic conclusion
- Too short

---

### AFTER (CNS-Quality, 144 words)

> "Aging is associated with stem cell decline, but whether this affects tumorigenesis remains poorly understood. Here, using physiologically aged mice, we demonstrate that aging suppresses lung cancer initiation and progression by degrading the stemness of alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells. Aged mice exhibited a 68% reduction in tumor number (n = 8 mice; P = 0.004, Student's t-test) and a 40% decrease in tumor volume (P = 0.012) compared with young mice. This phenotype is driven by aging-associated induction of NUPR1 and lipocalin-2, which together cause functional iron insufficiency. Genetic inactivation of the NUPR1–lipocalin-2 axis rescued stemness by 73% (n = 6 replicates; P = 0.002) and promoted tumorigenesis in aged cells. These findings reveal that aging-driven iron insufficiency cell-intrinsically suppresses both regenerative capacity and cancer susceptibility, with implications for regenerative medicine and cancer prevention in elderly populations."

**Improvements:**

- ✅ Specific gap ("whether this affects tumorigenesis remains poorly understood")
- ✅ Clear "Here we..." with experimental system
- ✅ Quantitative precision (68%, 40%, 73%)
- ✅ Complete statistics (n, P, test names)
- ✅ Specific mechanism (NUPR1–lipocalin-2 pathway)
- ✅ Causal validation (rescue experiment)
- ✅ Specific implications
- ✅ Appropriate length (144 words)
- ✅ All 8 structural elements present
