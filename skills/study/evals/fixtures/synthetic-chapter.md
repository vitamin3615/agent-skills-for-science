## [p. 101, §7.1, ¶1] The governing question

A population can grow rapidly when food, space, and other necessities are abundant. Yet sustained growth changes the conditions under which later growth occurs. This chapter asks: why does population growth often slow as population size rises?

<a id="loc-p101-s7-1-p2"></a>
**[p. 101, §7.1, ¶2]** As a baseline, suppose resources remain unlimited and each individual contributes the same average net number of descendants per unit time. The resulting model is exponential growth:

\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=rN,
\]

where \(N\) is population size and \(r\) is the per-capita net growth rate. Unlimited resources and constant \(r\) are simplifying assumptions, not claims that real environments remain unchanged.

<a id="loc-p102-s7-2-p1"></a>
## [p. 102, §7.2, ¶1] Density dependence

When individuals share a limited environment, rising density can reduce access to resources, increase exposure to waste or disease, or intensify competition. These mechanisms can lower births, raise deaths, or do both. Their combined effect is a decline in net per-capita growth as \(N\) increases.

<a id="loc-p102-s7-2-eq1"></a>
**[p. 102, §7.2, Eq. 7.1]** One compact representation is the logistic model:

\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right).
\]

Here \(K\), the carrying-capacity parameter, is the population size at which this simplified model gives zero net growth under the modeled conditions.

<a id="loc-p102-s7-2-p2"></a>
**[p. 102, §7.2, ¶2]** The factor \((1-N/K)\) scales net per-capita growth. At \(N=K/2\), the factor is one half; at \(N=K\), it is zero. Zero net growth does not imply that all births and deaths stop. Births and deaths may continue while balancing on average.

<a id="loc-p103-fig7-1"></a>
## [p. 103, Fig. 7.1] Two views of the same model

Panel A plots population size against time and shows an S-shaped path approaching \(K\). Panel B plots total growth rate against population size and shows a hump: total growth is zero at \(N=0\) and \(N=K\), with its maximum at an intermediate population size. The panels answer different questions and should not be read as interchangeable axes.

<a id="loc-p103-s7-3-p1"></a>
## [p. 103, §7.3, ¶1] A synthetic worked case

In a hypothetical closed culture, the modeled parameters are \(r=0.4\) per day and \(K=1{,}000\) cells. Equation 7.1 predicts total growth rates of 75 cells per day at \(N=250\), 100 at \(N=500\), and 75 at \(N=750\). These values are generated from the model for instruction; they are not experimental observations.

<a id="loc-p103-s7-3-p2"></a>
**[p. 103, §7.3, ¶2]** A close fit between observations and a logistic curve would show that the model describes the measured pattern under those conditions. It would not, by itself, identify which biological mechanism caused density dependence. Mechanism claims require additional measurements or experiments.

<a id="loc-p104-s7-4-p1"></a>
## [p. 104, §7.4, ¶1] Limits of the model

The logistic model treats \(r\) and \(K\) as fixed and assumes an immediate response to current density. Real conditions can alter resource supply and therefore alter the useful value of \(K\). Seasonal change, migration, age structure, and random events can also matter.

<a id="loc-p104-s7-4-p2"></a>
**[p. 104, §7.4, ¶2]** Delayed feedback can produce overshoot or oscillation instead of a smooth approach to \(K\). Thus, carrying capacity is best treated here as a model parameter tied to stated conditions, not as a timeless ceiling inherent in a species.

<a id="loc-p105-s7-5-p1"></a>
## [p. 105, §7.5, ¶1] Chapter argument

The chapter moves from an unlimited-resource baseline to a density-dependent model, interprets the model’s terms, separates pattern fit from mechanism evidence, and then identifies conditions under which the model becomes inadequate.

<a id="loc-p105-s7-5-p2"></a>
**[p. 105, §7.5, ¶2]** The preceding chapter introduced per-capita rates. The following chapter compares competition, predation, and resource depletion as mechanisms that can generate density-dependent patterns.

<a id="loc-p105-summary"></a>
## [p. 105, Summary]

Population size can modify later growth through density-dependent mechanisms. The logistic equation is a useful simplified model of that feedback, but its parameters, empirical fit, and biological interpretation must not be conflated.
